What dating apps
get wrong

Swipe apps are built for endless browsing. People join them to go on dates. Tender swaps the swipe for a rating, gives everyone one real plan a day, and puts her in charge of whether it happens.

The problemDating apps todayTender's bet

Swiping is shallow

A split-second decision on one photo.

She rates a dish he made. Effort, taste and personality in one video. She sees how he'd treat her at dinner.

Women get flooded

Anyone can message anyone, so inboxes fill with low-effort openers.

No cold messages. One plan a day, and only she can book it. She makes the call.

Dates feel like interviews

Two strangers, a drink, and pressure on the person.

Every plan is a meal or a class worth going to anyway. You're there for the experience, too.

Matches never become dates

Planning is left to the users, so the chat drifts and fades.

Every day there's a real plan with a time and a table. No chat that fizzles out.

Meeting a stranger feels risky

Plans are left to two people who just met online.

First dates are always at a restaurant or class. Home cooking unlocks only after you've met.

No reason to stay

The app is only useful while you're swiping.

Ratings, feedback and lessons make him a better cook either way. Men come back to get better.

Dinners,
not matches

Tender measures success by match-to-date rate: how many daily plans end with a booked table or a class. It's the number that matters to people, and the one most dating apps aren't designed around.

How Tender
makes money

Revenue comes from the meals and the skills the app creates, not from charging people to be seen.

Restaurant partners

Paid per seated table

Local restaurants join the curated list and pay when a Tender date is booked and seated. Couples who come back become regulars.
